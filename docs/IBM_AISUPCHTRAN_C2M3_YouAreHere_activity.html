Situation

Judge the next forecast change

You lead supply chain transformation at Sample Enterprise Goods, a manufacturer of household and personal-care consumables. An internal pilot forecast the Home Care line for two quarters. Nobody acted on its forecasts. Its summary reports that forecast error fell from 29% to 24%.

Before the next phase, the steering group wants to know how any change to the baseline forecast will be judged. Three functions each named the measure they trust. The group drafted one scorecard that combines them.

Forecast error
How far the forecast missed actual demand, as a percentage. Lower is better.
On time in full
The share of orders delivered on time and complete.
Composite
Each measure's movement multiplied by its weight, then added. A positive composite means the change helped overall.

What the scorecard left out

Three facts sat behind the three rows. Each was true before you chose.

Three measures. Three facts.

Two true numbers for on time

The 94% counts orders shipped by their ship date. The largest account counts delivery inside its appointment window, complete, and scores the company at 88% against a 95% requirement. It deducts on its own number.

A headline measured at two levels

The pilot's 24% measured the model across the national network. The 29% measured the current forecast at each location, where planners actually decide. Restated at one level, the gain is gone.

Inventory that cannot move on its own

A better forecast can shrink only the safety stock, a fraction of the line's inventory. That buffer is set in three places, by three different people. Unless all three settings change, it stays where it is.

Each measure was right for the function that chose it. A weighted total lets a gain on one buy back a loss on another, and weights still open at the readout invite each function to argue for the weighting it wins.

What your choice does

The capability this depends on

Each option was a reasonable move inside one scorecard. None began by fixing, before anyone saw a result, which measure judges the change, how it is calculated and against what, which costs it may not push onto another function, and what result would force a stop.

That is a paired measure set with a decision criterion: one primary measure, guardrails that hold the costs it could shift, and a go-or-stop rule, signed before the readout. It has to be in place before any forecasting change can be piloted, scaled, or funded.
