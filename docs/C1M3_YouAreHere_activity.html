Rank a set of proposals the way you would in any operations review. Then see what the ranking could not show. There is no right pick.

The situation

You are the supply chain transformation lead at Sample Enterprise Goods. The executive committee approved USD 9.5M for AI work over 18 months and asked for a prioritized plan. Before the plan, the steering group wants to know where AI creates value. Its sponsors sent eight proposals.

The benefits are not the same kind of number

ProposalBenefit as submittedWhat kind of measure it is

Revenue is not contribution, and a one-time balance is not an annual saving. A single dollar column would hide both facts, and three of the benefits have no figure to convert.

A loss with no proposal aimed at it

The books already show what the supply chain loses each year.

LossUSD per year
Premium freight21.0M
Excess and obsolete write-offs18.2M
Retailer compliance deductions (6.1M from the largest account)14.6M
Inventory carrying cost63.1M

One decision sits behind who absorbs those deductions: allocation under shortage. When stock runs short, the key account director and the senior vice president, supply chain weigh contractual priority, margin, and service to smaller accounts one event at a time. Nobody has written down how those three trade off. None of the eight proposals sets out to write that rule.

The capability this calls for

A ranking of proposals starts one step too late. First you map the recurring decisions: where each one loses money, and whether any form of AI could change the choice. Then you decide what to rank.
